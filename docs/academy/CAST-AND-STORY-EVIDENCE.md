# Cast and story evidence

This is the first-name-only, release-safe synthesis of owner-supplied class chats. Raw messages, contact metadata, private photographs, exact itineraries, workplaces, addresses, and health or relationship details stay outside the repository and never become dialogue.

## Named extended ensemble

### Nanako

Nanako is warm, concise, bilingual, and attentive to small social moments. She thanks people, apologises plainly when delayed, celebrates other people without fuss, and answers teasing with gentle humour. Her Japanese sounds lived-in rather than instructional.

- Learning home: natural short messages, social repair, pragmatic Japanese, and the difference between a technically correct phrase and one someone would actually send.
- Recurring bit: the group presents a dubious “Japanese” idea; Nanako quietly corrects it, and everyone treats the ruling as final.
- Recognition bond: the learner helps close an after-class exchange with the right degree of warmth.
- Friction bond: a vague group plan creates crossed assumptions; the learner must clarify without turning the chat formal or cold.
- Support bond: Nanako returns to a changed class and asks the learner to help a newer student join the conversation.
- Story use: an occasional returning friend whose presence gives invitations, timing, reunion, and natural-message tasks a human reason. Do not reproduce a real itinerary.

### Karen

Karen is friendly, direct, playful, and practical. She joins plans readily, handles logistics without drama, keeps in touch after time away, and mixes beginner Japanese with casual English. She is motivated by Japanese meetups and game-based learning, keeps a modest daily study habit, and notices when a learning game has run out of useful vocabulary.

- Learning home: habit design, beginner conversation, practical planning language, game vocabulary, and personal-corpus review.
- Recurring bit: she tests every study game until she finds its vocabulary ceiling, then asks where the rest of Japanese is hiding.
- Recognition bond: the learner welcomes her into the group and resolves a simple plan in clear Japanese.
- Friction bond: a game score says she is progressing while a real conversation feels harder; the learner helps separate streaks, known words, and usable language.
- Support bond: after an absence, Karen restarts with a small daily routine and invites another learner to do the same without guilt.
- Story use: a distant class friend who re-enters through the group chat, then becomes present in the Academy world. Do not reproduce real travel, payment, or health details.

## Ensemble chemistry worth preserving

- The group alternates practical class questions with affectionate teasing. Nobody needs a speech to prove they care; they share a tool, check a plan, send a photo, or make room at the table.
- Study arguments are small and useful: whether a word is “known” without its kanji reading, whether a game teaches enough vocabulary, and which tool helps without replacing practice.
- Missed lessons and late arrivals are met with humour and a way back in, not guilt. This supports the Academy return loop.
- Food, games, travel memories, and language meetups create reasons to use Japanese. They should remain ordinary conversation, not exposition or trivia.
- Group-chat scenes should be short, visually scannable, and task-bearing: clarify a time, soften a correction, repair an assumption, compare two study choices, or welcome someone back.

These are authored composites, not claims about real events:

1. A reunion plan fractures across three half-finished messages. The learner reconstructs who can come, corrects one assumption, and writes the message that makes the plan feel welcoming.
2. Karen finds the edge of a vocabulary game. Tom turns it into a challenge, Henry proposes a tool, and Nanako asks which words anyone can actually use. The learner builds a five-item personal-corpus round and explains the selection.
3. A novelty menu item is confidently described as Japanese. Nanako's mild correction sends the class into an evidence hunt that grows from N5 food language into an N2 source-comparison scene.
4. A classmate returns after a long absence. The chat begins with jokes, but the playable task is a low-pressure recap: one thing remembered, one thing changed, one next invitation.
5. A shared travel photograph produces incompatible captions. At higher levels the learner separates observation, inference, and memory without revealing a real place or private photograph.
