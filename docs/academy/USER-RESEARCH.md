# Yomu Academy Design-Thinking Workshop

**Status:** decision-ready simulated research. This is a hypothesis-generating workshop, not evidence about real learners.

## Product Decision

Yomu Academy should make its campus-life visual novel the default product frame and primary navigation. The campus gives every practice task a place, purpose, and return point. It must never become a tollgate: a persistent **Continue learning** control takes the learner straight to their next core activity from every campus, scene, recap, and settings surface.

The resulting experience is story-first in presentation and learning-first in consequence:

- The learner lands in a lived-in Bloomsbury campus, not a conventional course dashboard.
- A location, short scene, and practical reason frame the next Japanese task.
- Story progression, bonds, collectibles, and scene choices are optional. Skipping them does not remove, delay, or weaken a learning activity, review, placement route, or accessibility setting.
- Captions, audio-off, reduced-motion, keyboard, touch, pause, skip, and recap controls are product foundations, not premium or later extras.

## Scope, Ethics, and Evidence Limits

This workshop models plausible needs so the team can make an initial vertical slice deliberately. It does **not** claim that invented people are real users or that a fictional campus story has been validated.

### Real classmates are not source material

Real classmates are pending-consent, first-name-only inspiration at most. Until an individual has explicitly opted in to a defined use:

- Do not put any real name in Academy content, research notes, tests, assets, prompts, or analytics.
- Do not copy or infer contact details, attendance, enrolment, biography, appearance, voice, accessibility needs, personality, relationships, or any other sensitive trait.
- Do not make a one-to-one character, dialogue, likeness, or plotline from a real person.
- Do not use voice recordings, photographs, messages, or classroom material as reference assets.

The ten profiles below are wholly fictional composites. Their names and situations do not identify, describe, or stand in for classmates. Any future research must use a separate consent record, a stated purpose, voluntary participation, and a deletion path.

### Content and rights boundary

All narrative, Japanese dialogue, items, audio, art direction, and characters must be original Yomu Academy work or have explicit cleared rights. Do not import third-party game, anime, visual-novel, coursebook, or classroom characters, music, plots, item names, visual assets, or recognisable style imitation. UCL and Bloomsbury are geographic anchors only; no affiliation or endorsement is implied.

## Workshop Frame

**Decision question:** How might Yomu Academy make a learner want to return to a Japanese practice task through an ever-present campus story while preserving agency, accessibility, direct learning access, and privacy?

**Non-negotiable constraints**

1. The campus visual novel is the primary home and navigation model.
2. A direct path to the next core learning task exists on every screen.
3. Placement and dialogue support a learner from N5 through N4 without shaming a false beginner, heritage listener, or returning learner.
4. No core task requires sound, motion, a microphone, a personal disclosure, a social interaction, or a timed scene.
5. Story choices never act as hidden assessment, profile learners, or change access to grammar, review, or placement.
6. Progress, access preferences, and optional recordings stay local by default. Story interaction does not create an analytics dossier.
7. Every shippable asset carries rights provenance and uses original content.

## Simulated Workshop Method

| Phase | Time | Prompt | Tangible output | Bias control |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| Frame the challenge | 5 min | What makes a Japanese task worth returning to after a busy day? | Shared success definition | Treat story interest and learning value as separate signals. |
| Read the persona cards | 15 min | What is this learner trying to do before, during, and after a session? | Need, barrier, and opportunity notes | Use the full set of ten; do not design only for the most confident learner. |
| Map the journey | 15 min | Where can the learner lose control, confidence, time, access, or privacy? | Journey risks and recovery points | Include audio-off and reduced-motion routes as first-class journeys. |
| Write opportunity prompts | 10 min | How might we remove the barrier while retaining a vivid campus frame? | Opportunity statements | Do not solve a privacy problem with more data. |
| Generate features | 15 min | What is the smallest feature that changes the journey? | Bounded feature inventory F01-F22 | Separate content, interface, access, and data ideas. |
| Score independently | 20 min | Does this help, teach, burden, cost, or expose the learner? | Five-axis score per idea | Use lower scores for cognitive load and effort. |
| Sequence and challenge | 10 min | What must exist for a safe, accessible first slice? | Now, Next, Later decision | A charming story feature cannot outrank a core access requirement. |

The score table is deliberately a constrained decision aid. It is not a substitute for usability study, language-learning outcome research, or an accessibility review with people who use the relevant support.

## The Ten Synthetic Japanese Learner Personas

There are exactly ten learner personas in this workshop. Each is a fictional design instrument, not a profile of a real person.

| ID | Fictional context | Need | Accessibility requirement | Motivation | Failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P01, Avery | New N5 learner with a 12-minute bus ride and a phone used one-handed between commitments. | Know the next useful action immediately and finish a small task without rebuilding context. | Large touch targets, visible focus, and a pause that saves the exact beat. | Use a simple greeting and make a plan at an upcoming exchange event. | A decorative map with no clear next task turns a short break into browsing, then abandonment. |
| P02, Dae | Returning learner who understands familiar spoken Japanese but reads kana slowly after a long gap. | Receive a placement route that respects uneven knowledge instead of labelling them beginner or advanced. | Adjustable furigana and script support that can change by activity. | Turn passive recognition into confident reading and replying. | A one-score placement test places them too high, then basic literacy gaps feel embarrassing. |
| P03, Harper | N5 learner working irregular shifts, often studying late with sound muted and intermittent connectivity. | Complete equivalent listening work without downloading or playing audio. | Captions, timed transcript, visual speaker labels, offline-safe state, and no autoplay. | Make small daily progress without arranging a special study session. | Audio-first scenes or a lost session after an interruption make the product feel incompatible with life. |
| P04, Inez | Learner with strong household listening familiarity and novice literacy who prefers not to explain their background. | Choose support and difficulty without disclosing identity or being treated as an exception. | Per-line reading support and a route switch that does not erase progress. | Read practical notices and write clear messages independently. | Listening strength is mistaken for broad proficiency, so unfamiliar written material becomes a public-seeming failure. |
| P05, Kaito | N5 learner who uses text-first media and does not rely on sound. | Take part in every dialogue, including timing and speaker intent, through text. | Accurate captions, non-speech descriptions, speaker names, and visual alternatives for audio cues. | Follow a campus plan and contribute a written reply. | A lesson awards completion only after playback, or captions omit the information needed to answer. |
| P06, Mara | N5 learner whose attention varies across a crowded day and who returns in short, unpredictable intervals. | See a bounded task, restart safely, and avoid deciding among too many branches. | Calm hierarchy, a three-minute option, clear resume state, and no punitive countdown. | Build a reliable habit through several small wins. | A large map, urgent streak language, or a long scene overloads working memory before practice begins. |
| P07, Owen | N4 candidate who enjoys atmospheric fiction but experiences motion discomfort with camera movement and animated interfaces. | Keep the place and mood while controlling all movement. | Reduced-motion mode with static compositions, no auto-advance, and no parallax or screen shake. | Practice more natural planning and repair language in a calm setting. | Motion is required to understand a transition or triggers discomfort before the learning task begins. |
| P08, Priya | N5 learner reading on a laptop after class and needing more time to parse dense Japanese text. | Separate meaning, reading aid, and translation support rather than receive one overloaded view. | Text size, line spacing, contrast, furigana density control, and keyboard alternatives to drag actions. | Write a short polite message that another person can act on. | Dense text, crowded ruby, or drag-only ordering makes effort go into decoding the interface instead of Japanese. |
| P09, Sana | N4 learner using a shared device and a constrained data plan, cautious about personal data and recordings. | Study without an account, a social profile, cloud-synced choices, or mandatory microphone access. | Local-first storage, explicit delete controls, and a text equivalent for voice practice. | Maintain an independent routine and prepare for real-life coordination. | A friendly story asks for personal details or uploads a recording without a clear choice. |
| P10, Tomas | N5 learner who wants to speak eventually but needs a quiet rehearsal before any performance-like interaction. | Practise a dialogue privately, retry safely, and decide when to use a microphone. | Text rehearsal, self-paced playback, local-only optional recording, and non-judgemental feedback. | Feel ready to begin a first small conversation outside the app. | A forced roleplay, public comparison, or harsh correctness state creates avoidance rather than practice. |

## Journey Synthesis

| Journey moment | Learner need | Campus-story response | Non-negotiable recovery |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Open the Academy | Recognise a familiar place without orienting through a dashboard. | Return to the same campus map, time of day, and compact task card. | A direct **Continue learning** action is visible before any scene interaction. |
| Arrive and place | Start at an appropriate challenge without proving an identity. | A short arrival scene frames a route card, then a no-stakes adaptive placement. | Change route manually at any time; retain completed work and support settings. |
| Choose a location | Understand why a task matters in the world. | A location presents one practical purpose, such as clarifying a time or making a rain plan. | Preview the language objective and expected time before committing. |
| Read or hear dialogue | Control input density and modality. | Dialogue is authored in N5, bridge, and N4 variants around one shared communicative intent. | Captions, transcript, furigana, translation, audio-off, and text-first controls work independently. |
| Make a mistake | Recover without a story failure or a loss of place. | A character offers a rehearsal prompt or clearer clue. | Retry, reveal support, or continue with a flagged review item; no scene lockout. |
| Leave and return | Stop on time and resume with confidence. | The campus journal records a one-sentence recap and the next practical action. | Resume at the exact beat or bypass the scene to the next core activity. |

## Design Principles From the Workshop

1. **Story is the runway, not the tollgate.** A place and purpose should invite practice; neither may delay a core task.
2. **Accessibility is a route, not a preference badge.** Audio-off, text-first, reduced-motion, keyboard, and touch flows must each complete the same learning goal.
3. **Adapt the language, not the learner's identity.** Use deterministic placement, self-selected supports, and authored variants; do not infer personal traits from play.
4. **Show the learning reason before the narrative consequence.** Every choice names its language purpose and any story effect in plain language.
5. **Treat recovery as practice.** A retry is a useful rehearsal, not a red mark, broken bond, or lost currency.
6. **Make the campus durable but quiet.** A campus should be pleasant to revisit without streak pressure, urgency, artificial scarcity, or compulsive collection loops.

## Opportunity Statements

- How might the campus make a three-minute Japanese task feel like a meaningful contribution to a shared day?
- How might a learner reach the next core task in one action while still sensing where they are in the story?
- How might placement recognise mixed N5 and N4 knowledge without asking for personal history?
- How might one dialogue carry the same intention at N5 and N4 without leaking unsupported grammar into an early route?
- How might captions and audio-off preserve dialogue timing, intent, and atmosphere without requiring sound?
- How might a support conversation feel warm while never requiring disclosure, roleplay, or a personal response?
- How might visual-novel rewards remain expressive without selling, withholding, or distorting learning access?

## Complete Feature Decision Matrix

This is the complete feature inventory generated by the workshop. No feature has been silently prioritised outside this table.

### Scoring rubric

- **D, desirability:** 1 = little learner pull; 5 = solves a widespread, high-value need.
- **LV, learning value:** 1 = decorative; 5 = directly improves practice, transfer, feedback, or persistence.
- **CL, cognitive load:** 1 = reduces or adds almost no burden; 5 = materially increases choices, memory, or processing demand.
- **E, effort:** 1 = small scoped change; 5 = major cross-system or operational work.
- **P, privacy:** 1 = high exposure or difficult to make safe; 5 = local, non-sensitive, and easy to explain.

**Now** is D >= 4, LV >= 4, CL <= 3, E <= 3, and P >= 4, plus any access or consent foundation. **Next** is valuable but depends on a foundation or has higher authoring effort. **Later** is deferred because it has weak learning value, high burden, or unresolved privacy risk; it is not a promise to build it.

## Now, Next, and Later Commitments

### Now: first shippable campus slice

Build one complete loop: arrival, access setup, no-stakes placement, one campus location, an authored N5/N4 dialogue pair, one core learning activity, optional scene skip, recap, review scheduling, and return to campus. This slice proves that the visual novel can be primary navigation without slowing direct learning.

The Now work is F01-F14, F16, F17, and F19. Its implementation order is:

1. Establish local state, rights checks, access profile, campus shell, and the persistent direct-learning control.
2. Build placement and manual level/support changes before branching narrative content.
3. Add one dialogue in equivalent N5 and N4 variants with captions, text-first mode, skip, recap, and a core activity.
4. Add the recovery and resume loop, then test every input and access route.
5. Add a single optional support conversation only after the no-story path is equally complete.

### Next: richer but bounded campus life

F15, F18, and F20 deepen the world after the first loop is proven. Collectibles should support recollection, not status. Local microphone rehearsal remains optional and cannot become a prerequisite for speaking progress. More locations require an authored language map and the same accessibility and rights checks as the first location.

### Later: social and real-person ideas stay deferred

F21 has a high operational and privacy cost that is not justified by the initial learning loop. F22 is not an implementation backlog item: it remains prohibited until a distinct consent, safeguarding, rights, and deletion process exists, and even then no one-to-one portrayal should be the default.

## Product Tensions and Resolutions

| Tension | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| Immersive campus story vs. fast study | Campus is always the visual home; **Continue learning** bypasses scene reading in one action. |
| Adaptive challenge vs. surveillance | Use short deterministic placement, learner-selected route and supports, and local progress. Do not infer identity or export behavioural profiles. |
| Warm cast relationships vs. pressure to roleplay | Fictional support conversations are optional, skippable, non-romantic, and never ask for a personal disclosure. |
| Rewards vs. learning integrity | Items and currency are optional recollection only. They never buy answers, extra attempts, accessibility, grammar, or progress. |
| Atmosphere vs. access | Captions, audio-off, reduced-motion, text-first, and screen-reader paths carry the full learning objective and no penalty. |

## Validation Plan Before Scaling

Run moderated, consent-based tests of the first vertical slice before committing to more locations or plot arcs. Recruit volunteers independently of classroom records and avoid asking for identity details that are not necessary for the session.

| Test | What to observe | Pass condition | Stop or revise when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Find the next task | Time from opening campus to activating **Continue learning** | At least 80% complete it unaided in 10 seconds or less. | Learners search the map or story card for a required task. |
| Placement dignity | Understanding of result, route switch, and support controls | Everyone can explain that placement is changeable and not a grade. | Any participant feels labelled, trapped, or exposed. |
| N5/N4 equivalence | Same communicative intent across variants | Learners identify the same practical goal, with challenge matched to route. | One variant leaks unsupported grammar or changes the task's meaning. |
| Audio-off completion | Caption/text route through dialogue and task | The learner completes the same outcome without sound or a microphone. | Any required detail exists only in audio. |
| Reduced-motion completion | Static route through scene, choice, and activity | No required meaning relies on animation or auto-advance. | Motion causes discomfort or obscures state. |
| Privacy comprehension | Understanding of local storage, optional recording, and deletion | Learners can state what stays local and decline recording without consequence. | A choice feels hidden, coercive, or unclear. |
| Story value | Whether campus framing clarifies why the task matters | Most participants can name both the practical situation and language goal. | Story is remembered but the language purpose is not, or story delays practice. |

Collect only notes necessary to improve the experience, aggregate them where possible, and delete raw session materials on the agreed schedule. A positive reaction to the story does not validate the teaching design by itself; check task completion, error recovery, and delayed recall separately.
